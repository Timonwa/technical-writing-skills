# Sponsorship

A brand pays to appear on your blog or newsletter: a sponsored slot in an issue, a whole sponsored issue, or a sponsored post. The rules below cover the label, the piece, the numbers and the price. They are the shape of the obligations in the regimes named, from their regulators' own pages; a publication with real exposure in a market confirms the specifics there, and this is not legal advice.

## The disclosure label

### What the regulators say

**United States — the FTC**

- **Disclose the connection.** Where a connection between the endorser and the seller "might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement", it "must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously" ([16 CFR 255.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.5)).
- **Clear and conspicuous means hard to miss.** "Difficult to miss (i.e., easily noticeable) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers"; online it must be "unavoidable" ([16 CFR 255.0](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.0)).
- **Labels for sponsored content.** Terms "likely to be understood include" "Ad," "Advertisement," "Paid Advertisement," "Sponsored Advertising Content"; "Promoted" or "Promoted Stories" are "at best ambiguous"; readers may take "Sponsored by [X]" to mean the sponsor funded but didn't create the content; logos and brand names alone are "not likely adequate" ([FTC, Native Advertising: A Guide for Businesses](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/native-advertising-guide-businesses)).
- **Placement.** The label goes in front of or above the headline ([FTC, native advertising guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/native-advertising-guide-businesses)). On a blog, a disclosure "is more likely to be seen if it's very close to, or – even better – part of, the endorsement"; one at the very top of the page outside the post, or at the bottom, may be missed, and one in the middle or at the end of a post "is easier to miss"; a hyperlinked disclosure is "easily avoidable" ([FTC, Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking)).
- **Wording.** "Ad:", "Paid ad", "#ad", "Advertising:" or "Advertisement" at the start "would likely be effective"; "Sponsored by XYZ" is clearer than "Sponsored" alone, and "Paid post by [brand]" or "Paid post for [brand]" "would be much clearer" ([FTC, What People Are Asking](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking)).

**United Kingdom — the ASA and the CAP Code**

- **Identifiable as marketing.** "Marketing communications must be obviously identifiable as such" (rule 2.1), and "must make clear their commercial intent, if that is not apparent from the context" (rule 2.3) ([CAP Code, section 2](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/02.html)).
- **Advertorials.** Publishers "must make clear that advertorials are marketing communications; for example, by heading them 'advertisement feature'" (rule 2.4, same source). Where a brand has paid and "also has editorial control" — approving the copy counts — the content falls under the CAP Code as well as consumer protection law; paid content without control still needs disclosing ([ASA, Recognising ads: Advertisement features](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-advertisement-features.html)).
- **Labels.** "Advertisement Feature", "Ad", "Advert", "Advertising", "Advertisement" and "Ad Feature" are "very likely to be considered acceptable" if shown upfront and prominently; because "sponsored" is "open to varied interpretation", the ASA advises against it (same source). It also advises against "Supported by", "Funded by", "Gifted", "In association with…" and abbreviations such as "sp", "spon" and "aff" ([ASA, Recognising ads: Social media and influencer marketing](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-social-media.html)).
- **Placement.** Upfront, noticed "before they click on anything or otherwise engage with" the content; never behind "See more" (same source).

**Nigeria — ARCON**

- **Prior approval.** The Advertising Regulatory Council of Nigeria requires adverts aimed at the Nigerian market to be approved by its Advertising Standards Panel before they're exposed, and treats sponsored content by creators and influencers as advertising ([Techpoint Africa, 2025](https://techpoint.africa/insight/arcon-fight-digital-advertising-nigeria/); [Rest of World, 2022](https://restofworld.org/2022/nigerian-influencers-government-approval/)). A Federal High Court ruling of 30 April 2025 upheld its authority over advertising on all platforms (Techpoint Africa). These come from news reports; ARCON's own text on it wasn't found — unconfirmed.
- **Commercial nature not concealed.** Under the Nigerian Code of Advertising Practice, the commercial nature of internet and electronic marketing "must not be concealed or misleading" ([DLA Piper, Data Protection Laws of the World: Nigeria](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=electronic-marketing&c=NG)). Which edition is in force is reported differently by different sources — unconfirmed.
- **No label wording found.** No ARCON rule on the wording or placement of a sponsored-content label was found — unconfirmed. Use the label below, and agree in writing who obtains the approval where the advert is aimed at Nigerian readers.

### The label to use

One label that meets the wording and placement above in each regime named:

- **Wording:** `Ad` or `Advertisement`, with the brand: `Ad: paid for by <brand>`; for a sponsored post in the UK, `Advertisement feature` heads it. Never "Sponsored" alone, "In association with", "Thanks to <brand>", an abbreviation or the logo alone.
- **Placement:** before the headline of a post, and at the top of a sponsored slot, inside it, never in a footer or behind a link. A whole sponsored issue says so in the opening line, and the subject line or preview text names the sponsor (common practice).
- **Look:** the same size as the body text or larger, in a colour that contrasts with the background; never greyed out or smaller than the text around it (FTC: "a simple-to-read font with a contrasting background").
- **Social posts** promoting a sponsored piece carry `Ad` at the start (`cw:social-media-posts`).
- **Record it** in the brief's **Disclosure** field, and keep it for as long as the piece is live.

## Writing the sponsored piece

- **In your voice, for your readers.** It follows `writing-guide.md`, not the sponsor's brand guide; the sponsor's messaging is input, not copy.
- **Only claims you can check.** What you've used yourself is first-hand; what the sponsor claims is attributed to the sponsor. A claim you can't check doesn't run.
- **One link, one action** — the sponsor's, as agreed; no extra tracking links you haven't been told about.
- **A slot is short and apart** — about 50–100 words with one link is the common shape for a slot in an issue (common practice), set apart from the editorial sections so a reader can tell where it starts and ends.
- **A sponsored post** follows `cw:article-standards` like any post, with the label before the headline.
- **The sponsor approves facts, not the voice.** Edits within the agreed rounds; a change that makes a claim wrong or removes the label is refused.

## The media kit

`media-kit.md` sits in the publication folder beside `profile.md` ([template](../../style-guide/references/publication-template/media-kit.md)): who reads it, the numbers, what a sponsor can buy, the rate card, past sponsors and results, and how to book.

- **Numbers are measured, never invented.** Every figure is read from the platform or analytics tool and dated. Before a sponsor pitch or a reply, ask for current figures if the kit's dates are more than a month or two old (common practice).
- **Open rate is directional.** Privacy features that pre-fetch images inflate it (`cw:newsletter-standards`); lead with subscribers and click rate.
- **Past sponsors are named only with their permission.**

## Pricing

Set the price from your own costs and what your readers are worth to a sponsor, and record it in the rate card. Figures from others are context, never a rule:

- **Cost per thousand (CPM).** beehiiv reports newsletter CPMs "typically range from $10 to $75" depending on audience and niche, worked out as (price ÷ subscribers) × 1,000 ([beehiiv, 2025](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/newsletter-sponsorship-cost)). Some rate guides count CPM against opens instead of subscribers, so check the basis before comparing two figures.
- **No published range for sponsored blog posts** was checked — unconfirmed; price them from the slot's reach and the work.

## The results report

Send it within the time the agreement sets ([template](emails.md#sponsor-results-report)): the placement, the date, delivered, opens, clicks on the sponsor's link and the click rate, page views for a post, and what readers said. Every figure from the platform, with the date read; a figure you don't have is blank and said. Add the result to **Past sponsors and results** in `media-kit.md`.
