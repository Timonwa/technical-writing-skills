# Common problems in content, and a worked edit

The problems an edit of an article, essay, tutorial or newsletter issue keeps meeting, each with its fix, and one before-and-after to show the five levels working together.

## The problems

- **Throat-clearing intro** — three paragraphs on how common the problem is before saying anything. Cut to the first sentence the reader needed; keep a story only if it reaches its point quickly.
- **Missing nut graf** — a scene-setting opening that never says why it matters. Add the paragraph that does, by the second or third.
- **Headline that promises more than the piece delivers** — rewrite the headline to match, or deliver what it promised.
- **Unsourced or overclaimed figure** — "studies show…" with no study, or "proves" for a correlation. Find the original and match its certainty, or cut it.
- **Saggy middle** — five examples where two make the point. Keep the strongest.
- **Stacked quotes** — quote after quote doing the writer's job, with no analysis between them. Paraphrase most; keep the quote that says it best.
- **Ending that restates** — a final section that summarizes what the reader just read. End on the kicker, the callback or the takeaway.
- **Anecdote that never pays off** — connect it to the point or cut it.
- **Voice drift** — a piece that starts as "I" and slides into corporate "we", or changes tone halfway. Hold the person and tone the guide sets.
- **Missing or buried disclosure** — an affiliate link with the disclosure at the foot of the post. Put it where the reader meets the link.
- **A tutorial step that doesn't run** — a command that fails on a clean machine, or a sample that depends on a file the reader never made. Run it from scratch and fix the step, or the prerequisite.
- **Undefined term** — a word the reader doesn't know, used before it's explained. Define it where it first appears.
- **Curse of knowledge** — a draft from an expert that skips the step they stopped noticing years ago. Read as the named reader would, and find the leap.

## Before and after

An opening as drafted for a home-cooking publication whose guide sets a first-person, conversational voice:

```markdown
In today's busy world, baking bread at home has become more popular than ever. Many people want to try it, but it can seem intimidating. In this post, I'm going to share everything you need to know about overnight bread, which is basically the easiest method there is — only about ten minutes of actual work. I learned the hard way, too: I ruined three loaves before I realised my kitchen was just too cold.
```

Edited:

```markdown
I ruined three loaves before I worked out the dough wasn't the problem. My kitchen was too cold.

Here's the overnight method I use now: ten minutes of work tonight, and the loaf rises while you sleep.
```

What changed and why:

- **Substantive** — the point (what the reader gets) arrives in the second paragraph, and the piece now opens on the writer's own result, moved up from the end of the draft's paragraph, which is the evidence the method works.
- **Line** — the stock opener and "In this post, I'm going to…" cut as throat-clearing; "basically the easiest method" replaced by what's actually easy about it.
- **Accuracy** — "more popular than ever" dropped: an unsourced trend claim with nothing behind it.
- **Voice** — the first person kept, because it's the publication's voice, not a defect.
